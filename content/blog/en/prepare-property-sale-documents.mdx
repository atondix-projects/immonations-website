A property sale does not begin with the listing. It begins with a simpler question: which facts about the property can be documented today? Organising the records early reveals gaps before they surface during valuation, a buyer enquiry, or the buyer's financing process.

The aim is not to build the thickest possible file. The documents need to tell one consistent story. Floor area, construction date, plot, improvements, and registered rights should agree with each other. Any contradiction needs an explanation rather than smoother wording in the brochure.

## Start with an inventory

Collect everything that is already available. Separate current records from old copies and note where each item came from. A simple list with the columns "available", "check", and "missing" is enough for the first pass.

Typical documents for a house include:

- a current land register extract and cadastral map
- building plans and available approvals
- floor area calculation and floor plans
- energy certificate
- records of modernisation work
- property tax details

This is a working list, not general legal advice. The property type, its building history, and its use determine what else may be required. An extension, an annexe, or a change of use, for example, calls for a closer comparison between the plans and the official records.

## Do not copy floor area from an old listing

Floor area and the plan have a direct role in valuation and presentation. Yet figures are often copied from an old brochure, a construction drawing, or a private note. That is convenient, but it is not dependable if nobody can explain the source or calculation.

Check whether there is a traceable floor area calculation and whether the plan matches the property as it stands now. Alterations, combined rooms, or converted space can create differences between the drawing and the building. The [floor area and floor plans page](/en/living-area) explains how Immonation connects measurement, calculation, and sales documents.

## Add the owners association records for an apartment

An apartment is more than the rooms behind its front door. A buyer also needs to understand the shared property, ongoing costs, and decisions made by the owners association.

In addition to the property records, organise the following in particular:

- declaration of division and allocation plan
- minutes from recent owners meetings
- latest service charge statement
- current annual budget
- details of the maintenance reserve

Read these records before a prospective buyer asks for them. Decisions, planned work, or unresolved costs shape the conversation. An early review prevents a promising enquiry from failing later because of information that was available from the start.

## Request missing records deliberately

If something is missing, the whole preparation process should not pause while everyone waits for a copy to appear. Record which document is needed, which office or organisation holds it, and who will request it. A land register extract, for example, comes from the responsible land registry or through a notary. Other records may sit with the managing agent, building authority, or in the owner's own files.

Sequence matters. First identify the gaps that affect value and marketing. Then group requests and assign responsibility. Valuation work can continue from a clear interim record, provided that missing evidence remains visible rather than being treated as confirmed.

## Review valuation and documents together

A [property valuation](/en/valuation) is stronger when it considers more than the address and stated floor area. Condition, improvements, plot, specification, and relevant rights belong in the same review. Document checking should therefore happen before the price strategy is final, not as an administrative task at the end.

Online details can provide an initial indication. The sales strategy still needs a comparison with the actual property and the evidence available. If the floor area is unresolved or a plan no longer reflects the building, the valuation should show that uncertainty.

## A practical sequence for owners

1. Collect the available documents and sort them by source.
2. Compare core facts such as area, construction date, use, and improvements.
3. Review owners association records separately for an apartment.
4. Name missing or outdated evidence and assign each request.
5. Finalise the valuation, price strategy, and presentation from that record.
6. Approve the brochure and publication before marketing starts.

## Frequently asked questions

### Which documents do I need to sell a house?

Typical documents include a current land register extract, cadastral map, energy certificate, building plans, floor area calculation, records of modernisation, and property tax details. The initial review shows what is missing in each case.

### Which extra documents are needed for an apartment?

An apartment usually also requires the declaration of division, owners meeting minutes, the latest service charge statement, the annual budget, and details of the maintenance reserve.

### Can preparation begin while documents are missing?

Yes. Start by recording what is available, outdated, or still required. Missing records should then be requested deliberately before active marketing begins.
