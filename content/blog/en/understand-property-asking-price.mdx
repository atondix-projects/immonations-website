Anyone looking for an asking price can find a number quickly. Portals show nearby listings, calculators provide ranges, and an old brochure may still be in a drawer. The harder question is what any of those figures says about the possible sale of your own property.

A sound price strategy separates observation from assessment. It uses comparable data but also identifies where the comparison stops working. Two apartments in the same district may differ in floor, layout, condition, service charges, or shared property. For a house, the plot, improvements, energy condition, and practical use also matter.

## Do not confuse a listing with a completed sale

A price visible online is an asking price. It does not confirm whether the property sold or what a buyer ultimately paid. One listing should therefore be neither a price promise nor proof that a lower figure is wrong.

Use a broader set of questions instead:

- Is it the same property type?
- Are the immediate surroundings and connections genuinely similar?
- Do floor area, construction date, and condition match?
- Are the specification and improvements comparable?
- Are the documents complete and traceable?

The more answers remain open, the less weight the comparison can carry. Market data is still useful. It simply needs a clear frame.

## Read the price atlas as guidance

The [Immonation price atlas](/en/price-atlas) does not claim that one number is correct for every property. It shows price ranges and a median for covered districts, separated into apartments and houses. The figures are based on Immonation brokerage data. A range makes the differences between properties in the same location visible.

Start with the city, district, and property type. Then look at the spread as well as the median. A figure near the top or bottom of the range needs support from the property itself. Good specification on its own is not enough if floor area, rights, or necessary work remain unclear.

The atlas provides direction. It does not replace an inspection or document review.

## The features that change a comparison

An [individual valuation](/en/valuation) connects location data with characteristics that a table cannot fully represent.

### Layout and practical use

The same floor area can work in very different ways. Connecting rooms, sloping ceilings, natural light, and the position of ancillary space change how the property feels and functions. A current floor plan makes those differences easier to understand.

### Condition and improvements

Improvements do not all affect a sale in the same way. Their type, extent, date, and documentation matter. Invoices, descriptions, and plans help distinguish the current condition from an unmodernised comparison.

### Floor, plot, and immediate surroundings

For an apartment, the floor, lift, balcony, and shared property can affect the assessment. For a house, the shape of the plot, outside space, and possible uses are relevant. Roads, noise, orientation, and nearby buildings can vary even within one district.

### Documents and unresolved points

Unclear floor areas or missing records do not disappear because the asking price is optimistic. They usually return when a prospective buyer checks the property or prepares finance. Addressing these points early makes the price recommendation easier to explain.

## What an excessive starting price can do

A high figure does not automatically create room for negotiation. It may prevent suitable buyers from looking more closely. If demand remains weak, later reductions follow. Owners may then need to explain why the property has been listed for longer or why the price has changed more than once.

The opposite approach is not to set a low price without a reason. The task is to find a realistic position supported by the data and the property. That reasoning should still hold when a buyer asks difficult questions.

## From an indicative figure to a price strategy

A practical sequence is:

1. Place the location and property type in their market context.
2. Check floor area, plans, condition, and improvements.
3. Consider rights, costs, and the remaining documents.
4. Define the likely audience and marketing route.
5. Explain the recommended range, opportunities, and uncertainties.
6. Publish only after the documents and presentation have been approved.

The [property prices page](/en/property-market) provides the editorial market view for the region. The price atlas adds a district level view. The final decision then comes from the property itself, not from an isolated table.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is the highest asking price nearby a useful benchmark?

No. A listing shows what a seller is asking, not the completed sale price. A comparison only becomes useful when property type, location, condition, floor area, and specification are considered.

### Does the price atlas replace a property valuation?

No. The price atlas shows ranges and median values for districts. A specific property also needs an assessment of layout, condition, specification, floor, plot, and immediate surroundings.

### When should the asking price be finalised?

Only after the main property details, documents, and marketing strategy have been checked. This gives the price recommendation a traceable basis.
